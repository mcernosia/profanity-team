# Pricing & Positioning for Profanity Creative

How to frame Profanity's value, price work correctly, and avoid the commodity trap.

---

## The Core Positioning Problem

Most 3D studios get hired as production vendors. They receive a brief, deliver files, collect payment, and never get invited to the next conversation. The client doesn't think of them between projects. There's no loyalty. Every project is a new bid.

Profanity is repositioning upstream: not just executing production, but helping shape the creative decisions that determine what gets produced. This is the difference between being a tool and being a partner.

**The upstream position means:**
- Profanity is involved before the brief is written
- Profanity's perspective influences the creative direction
- The client thinks of Mark when they have a problem to solve, not just a file to produce
- The relationship has compounding value — each project makes the next one better

**How you get there:** Not by pitching "strategic partnership" (nobody buys that as a cold pitch), but by demonstrating it at every touchpoint. A pitch that shows understanding of their brand challenge. An email that opens with an observation, not a capability. A proposal that frames around outcomes, not deliverables.

---

## Pricing Philosophy

### The Commodity Trap

Day rates, hourly billing, and deliverable-count pricing are commodity signals. They invite the client to compare your rates to another studio's rates. You will lose on price to someone willing to race to the bottom.

**Avoid:**
- "We charge $X/hour for 3D rendering"
- "Price per asset" pricing at the proposal stage
- Itemizing small tasks (breaks the value frame)

**Instead:**
- Price the outcome and the relationship
- Use project-based or retainer pricing
- Reference comparable brand work, not time estimates
- Anchor on what good work generates (sales, brand equity, internal efficiency) before revealing a number

### The Anchoring Sequence

Before any number comes out of your mouth or into a proposal:
1. Establish that this is strategic work, not production
2. Reference what the client is trying to achieve
3. Surface the cost of the problem they have (inconsistent imagery, slow timelines, expensive shoots)
4. Then introduce Profanity's approach as the solution — and price the solution

If you jump to numbers before establishing value, the only thing the client has to evaluate is whether the number feels high or low.

## Pricing Tiers

### One-Off Project Work

**When to use it:** New client, proving the relationship, specific campaign or product launch.

**Typical range (as of 2025-2026):**
- Small product campaign (5–15 hero assets, 1–2 products): $8K–$18K
- Mid-size campaign (20–40 assets, multiple SKUs, varied backgrounds): $20K–$45K
- Large seasonal campaign (60+ assets, full art direction collaboration, multiple rounds): $50K+

**How to frame it:** Not by the asset count. By what the campaign is doing for them and what a comparable photo shoot would cost (photo shoots for outdoor/lifestyle brands run $15K–$100K+ once you factor location, talent, crew, post).

**Negotiating notes:** If they push back on price, before dropping your number, offer to scope down — fewer SKUs, fewer backgrounds, fewer revision rounds. Never discount the per-asset rate; it resets expectations for every future project.

### Retainer / Embedded Model

This is the upstream play. Profanity is a standing creative resource — on call for campaigns, product launches, sell-in materials, one-off requests.

**When to propose it:**
- After a successful first project
- When the brand has ongoing visual content needs (seasonal brands, high-SKU counts)
- When you've identified that they're spending reactively on visual content (last-minute shoot bookings, inconsistent output)

**Typical structure:**
- Monthly retainer: $6K–$15K/month depending on scope and deliverables
- Includes: X hours of active production, creative consultation, priority turnaround, quarterly strategy call
- Contract term: 6–12 months minimum to make it financially sound for both sides

**How to frame it:** Not "we'll do X assets per month." Frame it as: Profanity becomes their embedded creative intelligence resource — they get consistent quality, faster turnaround, a team that understands their brand deeply, and a predictable content budget instead of project-by-project scrambles.

**What a retainer actually delivers (beyond production):**
- Reduced briefing overhead (Profanity knows the brand, doesn't need to be re-educated each project)
- Strategic input: Profanity can flag when a brief is chasing the wrong thing
- Consistency across the entire visual library
- Speed: no onboarding, no quoting, just execution

### Pre-Production / Creative Strategy Work

This is the purest upstream play — Profanity gets paid to be in the creative process before anything is produced.

**Services:**
- Visual direction development (moodboards, style frames, motion language)
- 3D treatment writing for campaigns
- Creative brief development
- Pre-visualization / style frame approval before full production

**Pricing:**
- Pre-pro / strategy engagement: $3K–$8K depending on scope
- Can be structured as a standalone engagement or as a phase-one gateway to a larger project

**Why this matters strategically:** Once Profanity has shaped the creative direction, they're the obvious choice to execute the production. The pre-pro work is both revenue and a pipeline accelerant.

## How to Navigate the "We Have an Internal Team" Objection

This comes up constantly. It's not a rejection — it's an invitation to clarify how Profanity is different from an internal team.

**The reframe:**
- "Internal teams are great for managing ongoing production — we work best as an extension of your team when you need elevated craft, speed on complex assets, or a creative perspective from outside your org."
- "We're not trying to replace your team. We're most useful when they're underwater, when you're launching something that needs to look exceptional, or when you want a second creative voice before committing to a direction."

**The honest take (for Mark internally):** Brands with strong internal creative teams are actually great clients. They have the budget, they know what good looks like, they can brief clearly, and they don't need to be educated on the value of high-quality visuals. Don't get discouraged by in-house teams — figure out where Profanity fits alongside them.

---

## How to Navigate the "We're Working with Another Studio" Objection

**The reframe:**
- "That makes sense — who you work with for production matters. We're not necessarily looking to replace anyone. But if there are moments where your current setup is slower or less flexible than you'd like, we'd love to show you what working with us looks like."
- A single project to compare quality and workflow is often the best argument.

**The honest take:** If a brand has a strong, happy relationship with another studio, it's a long game. Stay in their peripheral vision, put out work that makes them curious, and wait for a moment of friction with their current partner. It'll come.

## Clients to Walk Away From

Not every inquiry is the right fit. These are signs to proceed with caution or decline:

- **Price-first conversations:** If the first question is "what's your rate per asset?", they're shopping commodity. You can try to reframe, but read the room.
- **No creative ambition:** Clients who just want renders to "look like the product" with no interest in craft, context, or visual story are volume shops. Low margin, high churn.
- **Unrealistic timelines with no budget flexibility:** Rush work is fine — at a premium. Rush work at standard rates means you've trained them to expect it.
- **Scope creep culture:** One small ask turns into twenty. If the first project they offer you has a vague scope, that's a preview of every project.
- **No internal champion:** If you can't identify one person inside the brand who is excited about working with Profanity, there's no one to advocate for you when budget decisions get made.

The opportunity cost of a bad client is enormous in a lean studio. One misaligned project can consume three months of capacity that could have gone to the right relationship.
