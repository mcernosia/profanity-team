# Voice Guide — Profanity Creative

Detailed reference for tone, register, what to avoid, and what good looks like. Load this when you need specificity beyond the SKILL.md overview.

---

## The Studio Voice vs. Mark's Personal Voice

These are two distinct registers. Using the wrong one is immediately obvious.

### Studio Voice (Profanity Creative as a brand)

Used for: website, capabilities deck, case studies, client-facing materials, bio in pitch decks

**Characteristics:**
- Confident without being arrogant — states capabilities directly, doesn't hedge
- Irreverent edge — not trying to sound like everyone else
- Specific and concrete — "3D product visualization for outdoor gear" not "immersive brand experiences"
- Positions the studio upstream — partner in the creative process, not vendor fulfilling a brief
- Efficient — no throat-clearing, no "at Profanity Creative, we believe..."

**Example (bad):**
> At Profanity Creative, we're passionate about bringing your brand's vision to life through innovative 3D visualization solutions that help you stand out in today's competitive marketplace.

**Example (good):**
> We make gear look exactly as good as it actually is. Sometimes better.

**Example (bad):**
> We specialize in photorealistic 3D rendering and animation for consumer brands in the outdoor, lifestyle, and action sports verticals.

**Example (good):**
> Profanity is a 3D product studio. We work with outdoor and lifestyle brands that want their gear to look like it was shot on the best day of someone's life — without needing the weather to cooperate.

---

### Mark's Personal Voice

Used for: blog posts, Instagram captions, LinkedIn posts, email newsletters, anything that should sound like a person not a company

**Characteristics:**
- Self-aware and slightly self-deprecating — Mark doesn't pretend he has all the answers
- Casual but not sloppy — contractions, natural phrasing, occasional profanity when it fits
- Honest about the process, including the messy parts
- Has a point of view — not just describing, but thinking out loud
- The kind of writing that makes you want to meet the person who wrote it

**Example (bad):**
> After 10 years in the industry, I've learned that the key to successful 3D visualization is a strong creative brief and clear communication with your client.

**Example (good):**
> The best thing anyone ever said to me about a project was "this looks wrong but I can't tell you why." That's actually useful. What kills you is "looks great!" followed by three rounds of revisions.

## The Outdoor / Lifestyle Space — What NOT to Write

This world has seen every cliché. If you write any of these, you've already lost the room:

**Phrases to never use:**
- "Born from a passion for..."
- "Where performance meets style"
- "Built for the relentless pursuit of..."
- "Engineered for adventure"
- "For those who refuse to settle"
- "Pushing the boundaries of..."
- "Gear for the modern explorer"
- "Built to last a lifetime"
- "Designed with the athlete in mind"
- "Where form meets function"
- Any variation of "chase your dreams"
- "Inspired by nature"
- "Because the mountain doesn't care"

**Why they fail**: They're abstract. They could apply to any product. They make no specific promise. The reader skims past them because they've read them a hundred times and learned nothing.

**What works instead**: Specificity. Concrete details. A specific moment, feeling, or use case. The thing that makes *this* product different from every other product.

**Bad**: Built for the relentless pursuit of the next summit.
**Good**: Made for the third day of a backpacking trip, when you're tired and your hands are dirty and you need it to work.

## Brand Copy for Clients — Register Matching

When writing for a client brand, match their register. Quick guide:

**Premium / Heritage brands** (think Arc'teryx, Patagonia at its best):
- Quieter confidence. Don't oversell. The product speaks.
- Fewer words. More white space.
- Technical accuracy matters — if you say something specific, mean it.

**Mid-market performance brands** (REI house brand, Osprey, Hydro Flask):
- More approachable. More fun is OK.
- Features and benefits are fair game, but frame them around moments.
- Community/lifestyle angle resonates.

**Challenger / Lifestyle brands** (Cotopaxi, Kavu, newer DTC brands):
- More irreverent. More personality.
- Brand values can be in the copy explicitly (not just implied).
- Social-native tone often works.

**Action sports** (Burton, Lib Tech, etc.):
- The most irreverent. Real voice allowed.
- Credibility comes from specificity and insider knowledge, not hype.
- If it sounds like marketing, it's already wrong.

## AI Writing Tells — Never Use These

The fastest way to get this copy thrown out is to make it sound generated. These are the patterns that give it away. None of them appear in anything Dusty writes.

**Punctuation:**
- Em-dash (—): banned entirely. Use a comma, a period, or rewrite the sentence. No exceptions.
- Ellipses used as stylistic flair (fine if a voice actually trails off, not as a crutch)

**Opener phrases: never start a response or paragraph with these:**
- "Certainly!" / "Absolutely!" / "Of course!" / "Great question!"
- "It's worth noting that..."
- "It's important to note that..."
- "I'd be happy to..."
- "In conclusion," / "To summarize," / "In summary,"

**Transition words that read as generated:**
- "Moreover," / "Furthermore," / "Additionally," -- use "Also" or restructure the sentence
- "Nevertheless," / "Nonetheless,"
- "In today's landscape" / "In today's fast-paced world"
- "In the realm of"

**Hollow buzzwords to cut on sight:**
- "Delve into" -- ever, for any reason
- "Tapestry" -- used metaphorically
- "Seamlessly"
- "Revolutionize" / "Groundbreaking" / "Game-changer"
- "Leverage" (when it means "use")
- "Unlock" (when it means "access" or "achieve")
- "Navigate" (used metaphorically)
- "Showcase" -- just say "show"
- "Cutting-edge" / "State-of-the-art"
- "World-class"
- "Disrupt" / "Disruptive"
- "Holistic approach"
- "Paradigm shift"
- "At its core"
- "Synergy"
- "Robust" (when describing almost anything)
- Fake hype generally -- "amazing," "incredible," "phenomenal," "game-changing" used to inflate ordinary things

**Structural tells:**
- Paragraphs that are all exactly the same length -- vary it
- Sentences that are too clean, too balanced, too neat -- add some rough edges
- Never having a one-sentence paragraph -- sometimes one sentence is enough
- Transitions that are too smooth -- sometimes you just start the next thought

**Voice tells:**
- Too formal -- Mark uses contractions, casual phrasing, occasional profanity when it fits
- Over-hedging -- "it's possible that" / "one might consider" -- just say it
- Passive voice as a default -- who did the thing? name them
- Every idea balanced by a counterpoint -- real voice has opinions, not disclaimers

**The test:** Read it out loud. If it sounds like a generated summary of itself, rewrite it. If it sounds like something a real person would actually say to another real person, it's probably right.

## CFC vs. Profanity — never cross the streams

Mark runs two brands. The voice rules apply to both, but the **naming** does not.

- **Profanity Creative content** (studio site, studio social, client-facing): use "Profanity Creative" / "the studio" freely.
- **Cowork For Creatives (CFC / YouTube channel)**: NEVER name "Profanity Creative." Say "my studio," "my creative business," or just describe the work without naming the company. CFC is teaching content for creative pros; it's about the operational system, not the studio brand.

When in doubt about which brand a piece of content is for, ask before drafting.

## Editing Checklist

Before delivering any copy, run through this:

1. **Does the first sentence do any work?** If it's a warm-up, cut it.
2. **Can any sentence be shorter without losing meaning?** If yes, make it shorter.
3. **Is there a single claim that couldn't be made by a competitor?** There should be at least one.
4. **Does it sound like a person wrote it?** Read it out loud. If it sounds stilted, rewrite.
5. **Are there any outdoor/lifestyle clichés?** (See the list above.) Cut all of them.
6. **Does it make the reader feel something or do something?** If not, why does it exist?
7. **Is every adjective earning its place?** If you can cut it without losing meaning, cut it.
8. **Any AI tells present?** (See the AI tells list above.) Rewrite until there aren't.

## What "Positioning Upstream" Means in Practice

Profanity isn't a render farm. It isn't "a vendor." The studio brings creative thinking, not just execution. Copy should reflect this — even subtly.

**Execution framing (wrong):**
> We can handle your product visualization needs from concept to final delivery.

**Partner framing (right):**
> We get involved before the brief is finalized. That's usually where the good decisions get made.

In case studies and capabilities copy, look for opportunities to show thinking, not just output. What was the creative problem? What was the insight? What did Profanity figure out that made the work land?
